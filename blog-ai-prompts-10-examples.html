10 Practical AI Prompts for Work, Study and Better Drafts

A useful prompt names the task, supplies the right context and explains what a successful result looks like. The ten examples below are starting templates, not magic phrases. Replace the bracketed material with your own facts and permitted source content. For important work, ask the assistant to mark unknowns and review the output before sending, publishing or acting on it.

Updated 4 November 2026Research-based editorial guide

Prompts 1–3: understand and summarize

1. Source summary: Summarize [document] for [audience] in five points. Use only the supplied text. Preserve exceptions and list any unanswered questions separately. Add a source reference for each important claim.

2. Plain-language explanation: Explain [concept] to a beginner using one example. State the assumptions, define unfamiliar terms and finish with two questions that check understanding.

3. Meeting notes: From [transcript], separate decisions, stated action items and open questions. Include an owner or deadline only when explicitly stated. Mark missing fields as unknown rather than guessing.

Prompts 4–5: write and revise

4. First draft: Draft [format] for [audience] using this approved brief: [facts]. The goal is [action]. Keep the tone [tone], stay within [length] and do not add claims, testimonials or numbers absent from the brief.

5. Controlled revision: Revise this draft to [specific change]. Preserve all factual claims and keep the meaning of [critical passage]. After the revision, identify any requirement you could not satisfy. Compare the result with the original before using it.

Prompts 6–7: investigate and decide

6. Research plan: Break [question] into subquestions. For each, identify the kind of primary source needed and what evidence would change the conclusion. Separate verified facts, assumptions and unresolved points. If you search, provide links and relevant dates.

7. Decision matrix: Compare [options] using these constraints: [requirements]. Show trade-offs, missing information and the conditions under which each option is preferable. Do not assign numerical weights unless I provide them or you explain a proposed method.

Prompts 8–10: build, plan and check

8. Coding investigation: Here are reproduction steps, expected behavior and the actual error: [details]. Explain the likely cause, identify evidence needed and propose a bounded change. Preserve [constraints] and explain how to verify the original failure.

9. Project plan: Turn [goal] into steps with dependencies, deliverables and decision points. Separate facts from estimated durations. Ask for the most important missing constraint before making a commitment.

10. Critical review: Check [draft] against [brief or source]. Flag unsupported claims, contradictions and missing requirements. Explain each issue with a specific passage; do not invent objections merely to fill a list.

Adapt the template and inspect the result

The important ingredients are the task, input, audience, constraints and acceptance check. A role such as expert writer can add context, but it does not supply evidence or make an answer correct. Provide the material that actually matters.

When a result fails, diagnose the missing context or unclear requirement before adding a longer list of instructions. Keep a record of prompts that work for repeated tasks, along with the input format and review process. Remove private details from examples you share with other people.

Frequently asked questions

Does a longer prompt always work better?

No. Relevant context and clear requirements help; extra instructions can conflict or obscure the task. Start concise and add what the failure reveals is missing.

Can a prompt prevent all hallucinations?

No. Requiring sources and unknowns can make checking easier, but important claims still need verification against evidence.

Can I reuse these examples in any assistant?

The general structures are portable, but tools, uploads and search access vary. Adapt the prompt to the capabilities actually available.
